## ACS Tables
The American Community Survey, produced by the Census Bureau, carries out 3.5 million surveys each year and is the leading survey informing federal policy. It releasese three data products: 1 year estimates, 1 year supplemental estimates, and 5 year estimates. These three products represent geographies of 65,000 for the 1-year estimates, geographies of 20,000 for the 1-year supplemental estimates, and drill down to neighborhood-level and rural counties for the 5-year estimates. In this project, I will use the 5-year estimates. The ACS ask questions regarding a wide area of topics in categories of social, economic, demographic, and housing.

## Housing Inventory Count (HIC)
The Housing Inventory Count (HIC) is a snapshot of all the programs within a Continuum of Care (CoC) that offer beds and units specifically for people experiencing homelessness. For permanent housing projects, this includes people who were homeless when they first entered, based on HUD's definition. There are five program types, each with its specific count: Emergency Shelter, Transitional Housing, Rapid Re-Housing, Safe Haven, and Permanent Supportive Housing (Point-in-time count and housing inventory count - hud exchange).

## Point-in-Time Count (PIT)
The Point-in-Time (PIT) Count is a one-night count conducted in January that provides a snapshot of how many people are experiencing homelessness, both sheltered and unsheltered. HUD requires Continuums of Care (CoCs) to conduct this count each year for people staying in emergency shelters, transitional housing, and Safe Havens. In addition, CoCs must count unsheltered individuals every other year (in odd-numbered years). Each community is responsible for planning, organizing, and carrying out the count locally (Point-in-time count and housing inventory count - hud exchange)

## Aggregating and Merging Datasets
The Jupyter notebook merge_datasets.ipynb includes a multi-step workflow for merging and aggregating three distinct datasets. The Point-in-Time (PIT) counts, Housing Inventory Count (HIC) data, and American Community Survey (ACS) data are merged into one single dataset. The core challenge is addressing the mismatch between the geographic boundaries of the CoC (Continuum of Care) areas (used by the PIT and HIC data) and the GEOID/County FIPS areas (used by the ACS data).

To resolve this geographic discrepancy, the notebook first reads a separate county_coc_match.csv file that serves as a bridge between County FIPS codes and CoC numbers. Using the networkx library, it constructs a network graph in which edges connect the associated County FIPS and CoC Numbers. This network is then analyzed to identify 347 connected components, or "Groups," each representing a collection of counties and CoCs whose boundaries overlap or are coterminous. A lookup map is created to assign a unique Group_Number to every County GEOID and CoC Number in the three primary datasets.

The final stage of the notebook involves aggregating the data by the newly created Group_Number and year. The ACS data is aggregated using summation for numerators and mean for central tendencies. The PIT and HIC datasets (which contain homeless and bed counts) are aggregated by summation. Finally, these three aggregated dataframes are merged on Group_Number and year to form the Final_Data_Set. The notebook then calculates final rates using the aggregated numerators and denominators, and saves the resulting unified dataframe to a CSV file.

# Methods
## Extra Trees Regressor
The Extra Trees Regressor, an acronym for Extremely Randomized Trees, stands as a powerful and efficient ensemble learning method within the domain of machine learning, primarily designed for regression tasks. It belongs to the family of tree-based models, sharing roots with the widely-used Random Forest, yet distinguishing itself through a critical increase in the level of randomization.

Like other ensemble methods, the Extra Trees Regressor constructs a large number of independent decision trees and aggregates their individual predictions, typically through averaging, to produce a final, more robust output. Its two defining characteristics are the use of the entire training dataset (rather than bootstrapped subsets) to train each tree and, more importantly, the introduction of extreme randomness in the splitting process.

When an Extra Trees model searches for the best way to split a node, it does not laboriously seek the optimal threshold for each feature. Instead, it randomly selects split points for a feature and evaluates only those random candidates. This radical randomization serves two key purposes: it significantly reduces the variance of the model, making it more resistant to overfitting, and it dramatically accelerates the training speed compared to methods that require an exhaustive search for optimal splits. Ultimately, the model achieves high predictive accuracy by sacrificing a small degree of local optimality for a massive gain in global model diversity and computational efficiency.

## Light Gradient Boosting Machine
The Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) is a modern, highly efficient, and distributed framework for implementing Gradient Boosting Decision Trees (GBDT), originally developed by Microsoft. It is designed to handle large-scale data and high-dimensional features with superior speed and lower memory consumption compared to traditional GBDT implementations.

LightGBM achieves this remarkable efficiency through several key innovations. The first is its use of a histogram-based algorithm, which buckets continuous feature values into discrete bins. This drastically reduces the computational cost of finding the best split points during tree construction, making the training process much faster.

Secondly, LightGBM employs a leaf-wise tree growth strategy rather than the level-wise (depth-first) growth used by most other algorithms. It always chooses to split the leaf that promises the largest reduction in loss, leading to a deeper, often more complex, and potentially more accurate tree structure. While this can increase the risk of overfitting on small datasets, it significantly accelerates convergence on large ones.

Finally, LightGBM introduces two feature-optimizing techniques: Gradient-based One-Side Sampling (GOSS) and Exclusive Feature Bundling (EFB). GOSS focuses on instances with larger gradients (the "hard" examples) to improve training efficiency without sacrificing accuracy, while EFB bundles mutually exclusive sparse features together to reduce the effective dimensionality. These features make LightGBM a state-of-the-art solution for production-grade machine learning tasks.

# Results
The analysis of the Extra Trees Regressor model reveals clear systemic drivers influencing homelessness. The most significant feature for estimating the rate of homelessness is the Total Population of a given area. This foundational variable suggests that the sheer scale of a community is the primary factor impacting the total count of unhoused individuals.

Following population, the next most important features provide deeper insights into resource strain and economic vulnerability. The count of available beds in shelters and temporary housing is a critical predictor, highlighting the direct role of resource capacity in mitigating the crisis. Finally, renter-occupied rate and the rent-burdened rate—the percentage of households spending over 30% of income on rent—underscore the powerful effect of housing precarity and economic stress. These top features collectively indicate that homelessness is primarily driven by an imbalance between population scale, insufficient supportive infrastructure, and pervasive rental market instability.
